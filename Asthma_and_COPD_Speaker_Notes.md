# Asthma and COPD: Speaker Notes

**English for Medicine II, group presentation.** Three presenters, about 7 minutes each (21 minutes in total), then questions.

How to use these notes:

- The scripts are written for a calm speed of about 110 words per minute. If you finish early, slow down and pause after key words. Don't add new content.
- Words in [square brackets] are actions, not words to say.
- Practise the respellings out loud. The CAPITAL part of each word is the stressed syllable.
- “Your clock” is how long you have been speaking when that slide ends. Use it when you rehearse with a timer.
- Props that help: a few drinking straws (slide 5) and an empty or training inhaler (slides 19 and 20).
- The same scripts are in the PowerPoint notes under each slide (View → Notes, or Presenter View).

## Presenter 1: The Airways and Asthma

Slides 1–8 · 420 seconds (7:00)

### Slide 1: Asthma and COPD

**What to say:**

> Good morning, everyone. My name is Presenter 1, and these are my classmates, Presenter 2 and Presenter 3. Today we are going to talk about asthma and COPD. They are the two most common obstructive lung diseases. Our title is "When the air can't get out." In a few minutes, you will see why.

**Key terms to stress:** asthma (AZ-muh) · COPD (see-oh-pee-DEE) · obstructive (ub-STRUK-tiv)

**Time:** 30 seconds (about 54 words) · your clock: 0:30

### Slide 2: Two Patients, One Problem

**What to say:**

> Let's start with two patients. This is Neda. She is nineteen, and she is a university student. She loves volleyball, but after a game she coughs and wheezes. A wheeze is a high, whistling sound when you breathe. And this is Mr. Rahimi. He is sixty-six, and he is a retired taxi driver. He gets breathless on the stairs. Breathless means short of breath. So, both of them struggle to breathe, and both of them wheeze. But they have two different diseases. By the end of our talk, you will know which disease each one has, and why.

**Key terms to stress:** wheeze (WEEZ) · breathless (BRETH-lus) · struggle (STRUG-ul)

**Time:** 50 seconds (about 98 words) · your clock: 1:20

### Slide 3: Our Plan for Today

**What to say:**

> Here is our plan. First, I will explain how the airways work and what "obstructive" means. Then I will talk about asthma. After that, Presenter 2 will explain COPD. Finally, Presenter 3 will show how doctors diagnose and treat these diseases. At the end, there is a short quiz for you, so please stay with us!

**Key terms to stress:** diagnose (DY-ug-nohs) · quiz (KWIZ)

**Time:** 30 seconds (about 56 words) · your clock: 1:50

### Slide 4: The Airways: An Upside-Down Tree

**What to say:**

> To understand these diseases, we need to know the airways. Our airways look like a tree, but upside down. The trunk is the trachea, or windpipe. The trachea divides into two big branches, called the bronchi. One goes to each lung. The bronchi divide again and again into small branches, called bronchioles. And at the end, like leaves, there are millions of tiny air sacs. They are called alveoli. In the alveoli, oxygen goes into the blood, and carbon dioxide comes out. Breathing in takes muscle work. But breathing out is passive. The lungs simply spring back, like a balloon.

**Key terms to stress:** trachea (TRAY-kee-uh) · bronchi (BRONG-kye) · bronchioles (BRONG-kee-ohlz) · alveoli (al-VEE-uh-lye) · passive (PASS-iv)

**Time:** 55 seconds (about 100 words) · your clock: 2:45

### Slide 5: What Does “Obstructive” Mean?

**What to say:**

> The word "obstructive" comes from the verb "to obstruct". It means to block. In obstructive lung diseases, the airways become narrow. Look at the picture. At the top, a healthy airway is wide open, and the air flows out easily. At the bottom, the airway is narrow. Air can get in, but it is very hard to get out. So old air stays trapped inside the lungs. Now, let's try something together. Please take a deep breath in. Now breathe out slowly through a straw, or through pursed lips, like this. [Pause and demonstrate.] It takes a long time, right? Now imagine breathing like this all day. That is life with an obstructive lung disease.

**Key terms to stress:** obstruct (ub-STRUKT) · narrow (NAIR-oh) · trapped (TRAPT) · pursed lips (PURST lips)

**Time:** 65 seconds (about 112 words) · your clock: 3:50

### Slide 6: What Happens in Asthma?

**What to say:**

> Now, let's go back to Neda. Neda has asthma. Asthma is a long-term disease of the airways. Long-term means it lasts for many years. In asthma, the airways are very sensitive. When they meet a trigger, they overreact. Look at the two circles. They show an airway cut across. On the left, a normal airway is wide open. On the right is an airway during an asthma attack. Three things happen. Number one: the wall becomes swollen. We call this inflammation. Number two: the muscle around the airway squeezes tight. This is called bronchospasm. Number three: the airway makes too much mucus, a thick, sticky liquid. Together, these changes make the airway very narrow. But here is the good news. In asthma, the narrowing is usually reversible. Reversible means it can go back to normal.

**Key terms to stress:** sensitive (SEN-sih-tiv) · inflammation (in-fluh-MAY-shun) · bronchospasm (BRONG-koh-spaz-um) · mucus (MYOO-kus) · reversible (rih-VER-suh-bul)

**Time:** 75 seconds (about 135 words) · your clock: 5:05

### Slide 7: What Triggers Asthma?

**What to say:**

> So, what makes the airways react? We call it a trigger. A trigger is something that starts the symptoms. The most common triggers are allergens. An allergen is a substance that causes an allergy, like pollen from trees and flowers, dust mites in our beds, pet hair, and mould. Cigarette smoke and air pollution are triggers too. So are cold air and exercise. Colds and flu are very common triggers, especially in children. Even stress and strong emotions can start an attack. For Neda, the triggers are exercise and pollen in spring. That is why her symptoms come and go.

**Key terms to stress:** trigger (TRIG-er) · allergen (AL-er-jen) · pollen (POL-un) · dust mites (DUST mytes) · mould (MOHLD)

**Time:** 55 seconds (about 100 words) · your clock: 6:00

### Slide 8: Symptoms of Asthma

**What to say:**

> These are the four main symptoms of asthma. Wheezing, the whistling sound. Coughing, often at night or early in the morning. Chest tightness. Patients say it feels like a tight band around the chest. And shortness of breath. The key word for asthma is "variable". It means the symptoms come and go. But sometimes an attack is severe. Watch for these danger signs: the patient cannot finish a sentence, the lips turn blue, or the inhaler does not help. This is an emergency, so call for help immediately. That's all about asthma from me. Now, Presenter 2 will tell you what is happening to Mr. Rahimi.

**Key terms to stress:** variable (VAIR-ee-uh-bul) · severe (suh-VEER) · emergency (ih-MER-jen-see) · immediately (ih-MEE-dee-ut-lee)

**Time:** 60 seconds (about 106 words) · your clock: 7:00

**Total for Presenter 1: 420 seconds (7:00)**

### Questions the audience might ask Presenter 1

1. **Can asthma be cured?**
   Not yet. But with the right inhalers and by avoiding triggers, most people control it well and live a normal, active life.

2. **Is asthma contagious?**
   No, you cannot catch asthma from another person. But colds and flu are contagious, and they can trigger an asthma attack.

## Presenter 2: COPD

Slides 9–16 · 420 seconds (7:00)

### Slide 9: Back to Mr. Rahimi

**What to say:**

> Thank you, Presenter 1. Now let's go back to our second patient, Mr. Rahimi. He smoked one pack of cigarettes a day for forty years. Every morning, he coughs and brings up phlegm. Phlegm is the thick mucus we cough up from the chest. Ten years ago, he was breathless only on the stairs. Now he is breathless even on flat ground. His symptoms don't come and go like Neda's. They get slowly worse, year after year.

**Key terms to stress:** phlegm (FLEM) · cigarettes (sig-uh-RETS) · flat ground

**Time:** 40 seconds (about 77 words) · your clock: 0:40

### Slide 10: COPD, Letter by Letter

**What to say:**

> Mr. Rahimi has COPD. COPD is an abbreviation, so let's read it letter by letter. C is for chronic. Chronic means long-lasting. O is for obstructive, which means blocking the airflow. P is for pulmonary. Pulmonary means related to the lungs. And D is for disease. So, COPD is a long-lasting lung disease that blocks the airflow. Doctors and nurses use this abbreviation every day. Now, three key facts. First, the blockage is not fully reversible. This is the big difference from asthma. Second, COPD is progressive. Progressive means it slowly gets worse. And third, it usually starts after the age of forty.

**Key terms to stress:** abbreviation (uh-bree-vee-AY-shun) · chronic (KRON-ik) · pulmonary (PUL-muh-nair-ee) · progressive (pruh-GRES-iv)

**Time:** 60 seconds (about 103 words) · your clock: 1:40

### Slide 11: Two Types of COPD

**What to say:**

> COPD has two main types. Most patients, like Mr. Rahimi, have a mix of both. The first type is chronic bronchitis. The ending "itis" means inflammation. So bronchitis is inflammation of the bronchi, the big tubes. The tubes become swollen, and they make too much mucus. The main sign is a cough with phlegm that lasts for months. The second type is emphysema. Here, the problem is in the alveoli, the tiny air sacs. Look at the picture. Healthy alveoli are small, like a bunch of grapes. In emphysema, their thin walls break down. Many small sacs become a few big, floppy ones. The lungs lose their elasticity. That is their ability to stretch and spring back. So the air stays trapped inside. Remember: bronchitis is in the tubes, and emphysema is in the air sacs.

**Key terms to stress:** bronchitis (brong-KY-tis) · emphysema (em-fuh-SEE-muh) · floppy (FLOP-ee) · elasticity (ee-las-TIS-ih-tee)

**Time:** 75 seconds (about 136 words) · your clock: 2:55

### Slide 12: What Damages the Lungs?

**What to say:**

> So, what damages the lungs in COPD? The main cause is tobacco smoke. That includes cigarettes, the water pipe, or hookah, and second-hand smoke. Second-hand smoke means breathing in other people's smoke. According to the World Health Organization, in high-income countries more than seventy percent of COPD cases are caused by smoking. But in poorer countries, another big cause is household air pollution. This is smoke from cooking or heating with wood or coal inside the home. Polluted air, dust and chemicals at work can also hurt the lungs. Finally, a rare genetic condition can cause COPD in young people.

**Key terms to stress:** tobacco (tuh-BAK-oh) · hookah (HOOK-uh) · second-hand smoke · household air pollution (puh-LOO-shun) · genetic (juh-NET-ik)

**Time:** 55 seconds (about 100 words) · your clock: 3:50

### Slide 13: How COPD Feels Over the Years

**What to say:**

> The main symptoms of COPD are shortness of breath, a chronic cough, and sputum. Sputum is the medical word for phlegm. The medical word for shortness of breath is dyspnea. Many patients also feel tired all the time. Now look at the steps on the right. In COPD, breathlessness slowly gets worse. At first, the patient is breathless only on stairs or hills. Later, it happens when walking on flat ground. In severe COPD, even getting dressed is hard work. Many people think this is just "getting old", so they see a doctor very late.

**Key terms to stress:** sputum (SPYOO-tum) · dyspnea (DISP-nee-uh) · breathlessness (BRETH-lus-nus)

**Time:** 55 seconds (about 95 words) · your clock: 4:45

### Slide 14: Flare-Ups: When COPD Gets Worse

**What to say:**

> Sometimes COPD suddenly gets worse for a few days. We call this a flare-up. The medical word is exacerbation. The patient becomes more breathless than usual. They cough more, and the sputum can change colour to yellow or green. The most common causes are chest infections and air pollution. A serious flare-up needs hospital care, often with oxygen and extra medicine. Each flare-up can also leave the lungs a little weaker. That is why preventing them is so important.

**Key terms to stress:** flare-up (FLAIR-up) · exacerbation (ig-ZAS-er-BAY-shun) · infection (in-FEK-shun)

**Time:** 45 seconds (about 79 words) · your clock: 5:30

### Slide 15: Why Should We Care?

**What to say:**

> Why should we care about these diseases? Let's look at the numbers from the World Health Organization. In 2019, about two hundred and sixty-two million people had asthma. In the same year, asthma caused four hundred and fifty-five thousand deaths. Asthma is also the most common chronic disease in children. COPD is even more deadly. In 2023, it caused three point four million deaths. That makes COPD the third leading cause of death in the world. And most of these deaths happen in low- and middle-income countries.

**Key terms to stress:** 262 million (two hundred and sixty-two million) · 455,000 (four hundred and fifty-five thousand) · 3.4 million (three point four million) · leading cause (LEE-ding kawz)

**Time:** 45 seconds (about 87 words) · your clock: 6:15

### Slide 16: Patient Words, Doctor Words

**What to say:**

> Before we move on, here is something useful for us as future doctors. Patients and doctors often use different words for the same thing. When a patient says, "I can't catch my breath", the doctor writes "dyspnea". "I'm coughing up phlegm" becomes "productive cough with sputum". "A whistling in my chest" is a wheeze. "It suddenly got worse" is an exacerbation. And a "puffer" is an inhaler. Now, Presenter 3 will explain how doctors diagnose and treat these diseases.

**Key terms to stress:** productive cough (pruh-DUK-tiv KOF) · puffer (PUF-er) · inhaler (in-HAY-ler)

**Time:** 45 seconds (about 79 words) · your clock: 7:00

**Total for Presenter 2: 420 seconds (7:00)**

### Questions the audience might ask Presenter 2

1. **If a smoker stops smoking, can the lungs heal?**
   The damage that is already there does not fully go away. But stopping smoking slows down the damage at any age, so it is the most important treatment.

2. **Is the water pipe (hookah) safer than cigarettes?**
   No. Water pipe smoke has the same harmful chemicals, and one long session can mean breathing in much more smoke than one cigarette.

## Presenter 3: Diagnosis, Treatment and Review

Slides 17–25 · 420 seconds (7:00)

### Slide 17: How Do Doctors Find Out?

**What to say:**

> Thank you, Presenter 2. So, how do doctors find out if a patient has asthma or COPD? There are three steps. Step one: ask. The doctor takes a history. A history is the patient's story: their symptoms, smoking, allergies, and family illnesses. Step two: listen. The doctor listens to the chest with a stethoscope and may hear a wheeze. Step three: test. The key test is spirometry. Let's see how it works.

**Key terms to stress:** history (HIS-tuh-ree) · stethoscope (STETH-uh-skohp) · spirometry (spy-ROM-uh-tree)

**Time:** 40 seconds (about 72 words) · your clock: 0:40

### Slide 18: Spirometry: The Blowing Test

**What to say:**

> Spirometry measures how much air you can blow out, and how fast. The patient takes the deepest breath possible. Then they blow into a machine as hard and as fast as they can. Look at the graph. The blue line is a healthy person. In the first second, they blow out four litres. We call this FEV1. In total, they blow out five litres. This is FVC. Four divided by five is zero point eight. That's normal. The orange line is a patient with obstruction. The air comes out slowly. FEV1 divided by FVC is only zero point five. Below zero point seven means obstruction. Then the patient uses a reliever inhaler and repeats the test. In asthma, the result gets much better. In COPD, it improves only a little.

**Key terms to stress:** FEV1 (eff-ee-vee-WUN) · FVC (eff-vee-SEE) · litres (LEE-terz) · obstruction (ub-STRUK-shun) · reliever (rih-LEE-ver)

**Time:** 70 seconds (about 130 words) · your clock: 1:50

### Slide 19: Treating Asthma: Two Inhalers

**What to say:**

> Now, treatment. Most asthma medicines are inhalers. An inhaler is a small device that sends the medicine straight into the lungs. There are two main kinds. The first is the reliever. It works in minutes and relaxes the airway muscle. So we call it a bronchodilator, which means "airway opener". A common example is salbutamol. The second is the controller, or preventer. It contains a steroid, a medicine that calms the inflammation. It works slowly, so patients use it every day, even when they feel well. An example is budesonide. Remember: a reliever alone is not enough. The inflammation needs treatment too.

**Key terms to stress:** bronchodilator (BRONG-koh-dy-LAY-ter) · salbutamol (sal-BYOO-tuh-mol) · steroid (STEER-oyd) · budesonide (byoo-DES-uh-nyde) · preventer (prih-VEN-ter)

**Time:** 60 seconds (about 102 words) · your clock: 2:50

### Slide 20: How to Use an Inhaler

**What to say:**

> An inhaler only works if you use it correctly. So here are the steps, the way we explain them to a patient. One: shake the inhaler. Two: breathe out gently, away from it. Three: close your lips around it. Four: press once, and at the same time, breathe in slowly and deeply. Five: hold your breath for about ten seconds. Six: after a steroid inhaler, rinse your mouth. Did you notice the grammar? To give instructions, we use the imperative: shake, breathe, press, hold.

**Key terms to stress:** imperative (im-PAIR-uh-tiv) · gently (JENT-lee) · rinse (RINS)

**Time:** 45 seconds (about 84 words) · your clock: 3:35

### Slide 21: Treating COPD

**What to say:**

> Now let's look at COPD treatment. Number one, and the most important: stop smoking. It is never too late, and quitting slows down the damage. Number two: inhalers that open the airways, the bronchodilators. Number three: pulmonary rehabilitation. This is a programme of exercise and breathing training. It helps patients feel less breathless and do more every day. Number four: vaccines against flu, pneumonia and COVID-19. They help to prevent flare-ups. And number five: oxygen therapy, for patients with severe COPD. COPD cannot be cured, but treatment helps people breathe better and live better.

**Key terms to stress:** rehabilitation (REE-huh-bil-ih-TAY-shun) · vaccines (vak-SEENZ) · pneumonia (noo-MOH-nee-uh) · therapy (THAIR-uh-pee)

**Time:** 55 seconds (about 94 words) · your clock: 4:30

### Slide 22: Asthma vs COPD: Neda and Mr. Rahimi

**What to say:**

> Now we can answer our question from the beginning. Why do Neda and Mr. Rahimi have different diseases? Let's compare. Asthma often starts in childhood or youth. COPD usually starts after forty. Asthma comes from sensitive airways and triggers. COPD comes mainly from smoking and other smoke. In asthma, symptoms come and go. In COPD, they slowly get worse. After a reliever inhaler, the spirometry result improves a lot in asthma, but only a little in COPD. And the main treatments are different too. So, Neda has asthma, and Mr. Rahimi has COPD.

**Key terms to stress:** compare (kum-PAIR) · youth (YOOTH) · reversible (rih-VER-suh-bul)

**Time:** 55 seconds (about 93 words) · your clock: 5:25

### Slide 23: Quick Quiz: Asthma or COPD?

**What to say:**

> Now it's your turn! I will read four short cases. Please call out the answer: asthma or COPD? Number one. A nine-year-old boy wheezes every time he plays with a cat. [Pause, then click.] Yes, asthma! Number two. A seventy-year-old woman cooked over a wood fire for fifty years. Now she is breathless every day. [Pause, then click.] Correct, COPD, from household smoke. Number three. After a reliever inhaler, his breathing test is almost normal. [Click.] Asthma. Number four. The walls of her alveoli are broken, and air is trapped. [Click.] COPD. Well done, everyone!

**Key terms to stress:** call out (KAWL OWT) · household (HOWS-hohld)

**Time:** 55 seconds (about 87 words) · your clock: 6:20

### Slide 24: Take-Home Messages

**What to say:**

> To finish, here are our take-home messages. Both diseases block the air from getting out. Asthma comes and goes, and it is reversible. COPD comes mostly from smoke, and it slowly gets worse. Spirometry tells them apart. And the best prevention is simple: don't smoke.

**Key terms to stress:** take-home message · prevention (prih-VEN-shun)

**Time:** 25 seconds (about 45 words) · your clock: 6:45

### Slide 25: Thank You!

**What to say:**

> That's the end of our presentation. Thank you very much for listening, and thank you to our teacher. Now we are happy to answer your questions.

**Key terms to stress:** questions (KWES-chunz)

**Time:** 15 seconds (about 26 words) · your clock: 7:00

### Questions the audience might ask Presenter 3

1. **Why should patients rinse their mouth after a steroid inhaler?**
   Some of the medicine stays in the mouth and throat. Rinsing helps to prevent a fungal mouth infection, called thrush, and a hoarse voice.

2. **Can a person have both asthma and COPD?**
   Yes. Some patients, often older smokers who had asthma before, have features of both. Doctors then treat both the inflammation and the airflow blockage.
