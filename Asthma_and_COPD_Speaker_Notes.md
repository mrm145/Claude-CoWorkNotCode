## Presenter 1: Introduction, how we breathe, and asthma

**Slides 1–8 · Total time: 7:00 (420 seconds)**

### Slide 1: Asthma and COPD

**What to say:**

> Good morning, everyone, and thank you for being here. We are a team of three students. My name is Presenter 1, and these are my teammates, Presenter 2 and Presenter 3. Today, our topic is asthma and COPD. These are the two major obstructive lung diseases. Millions of people around the world live with them. Don't worry, we will keep it simple and clear. Let's begin.

**Key terms to stress:** **asthma** (AZ-muh) · **COPD** (see-oh-pee-DEE) · **obstructive** (ub-STRUK-tiv)

**Time:** 35 seconds

### Slide 2: Our Plan for Today

**What to say:**

> Here is our plan for today. First, I will explain how we breathe and what the word "obstructive" means. Then I will talk about asthma. Next, Presenter 2 will talk about COPD: what it is, why it happens, and how it feels for patients. Finally, Presenter 3 will explain how doctors diagnose and treat these diseases. At the end, we have a short quiz for you. So please listen carefully!

**Key terms to stress:** **diagnose** (DY-ug-nohs)

**Time:** 35 seconds

### Slide 3: How We Breathe

**What to say:**

> Before we talk about diseases, let's look at a healthy lung. When we breathe in, air enters through the nose and mouth. Then it goes down the trachea. The trachea is the windpipe, the big tube in your neck. The trachea divides into two bronchi. The bronchi are the large tubes that go into each lung. They divide again and again into smaller tubes, called bronchioles. At the very end, we find the alveoli. Alveoli are tiny air sacs. Here, oxygen goes into the blood, and carbon dioxide comes out. Breathing in needs work from our muscles. But breathing out is easy. The lungs simply spring back, like a balloon.

**Key terms to stress:** **trachea** (TRAY-kee-uh) · **bronchi** (BRONG-kye) · **bronchioles** (BRONG-kee-ohlz) · **alveoli** (al-VEE-uh-lye)

**Time:** 60 seconds

### Slide 4: What Does “Obstructive” Mean?

**What to say:**

> So, what does "obstructive" mean? The verb "to obstruct" means to block. In obstructive lung diseases, the airways become narrow. Air can still get in, but it is hard to get it out. So some old air stays trapped inside the lungs. Doctors call this air trapping. Let's try a small experiment. Imagine you take a deep breath, and then you breathe out through a thin drinking straw. It takes a long time, right? You need a lot of effort, and you get tired very quickly. This is how a patient with an obstructive disease can feel every day. The two main obstructive diseases are asthma and COPD.

**Key terms to stress:** **obstruct** (ub-STRUKT) · **airways** (AIR-wayz) · **trapped**

**Time:** 60 seconds

### Slide 5: Meet Sara: An Asthma Story

**What to say:**

> Now, let me introduce Sara. Sara is fourteen years old, and she loves football. But after running, she starts to cough. Sometimes she has to stop playing and sit down. She also wheezes. A wheeze is a high, whistling sound when you breathe out. Her chest feels tight, and it is often worse at night. But here is the strange part. On other days, she feels completely fine. So, what is happening in her lungs?

**Key terms to stress:** **wheeze** (WEEZ)

**Time:** 40 seconds

### Slide 6: What Is Asthma?

**What to say:**

> Sara has asthma. Asthma is a long-term disease of the airways. Long-term means it lasts for years, sometimes for life. In asthma, the airways are very sensitive. When they meet a trigger, they react strongly, and three things happen. Let's look at them on the slide. First, swelling. The airway wall becomes inflamed. Inflammation means the tissue is red, swollen and irritated. Second, tightening. The small muscles around the airway squeeze. We call this bronchospasm. Third, mucus. Mucus is a thick, sticky liquid, and the airway makes too much of it. Together, these changes make the tube very narrow. But there is good news. In asthma, this narrowing is usually reversible. Reversible means it can go back to normal. With treatment, the airways can open again, and the patient can breathe normally.

**Key terms to stress:** **inflammation** (in-fluh-MAY-shun) · **bronchospasm** (BRONG-koh-spaz-um) · **mucus** (MYOO-kus) · **reversible** (rih-VER-suh-bul)

**Time:** 75 seconds

### Slide 7: Common Asthma Triggers

**What to say:**

> We said that asthma reacts to triggers. A trigger is something that starts the symptoms. Triggers are different for each person. The most common ones are allergens. An allergen is a substance that causes an allergy, like pollen from flowers, dust mites in our beds, pet hair, or mould. Other triggers are cigarette smoke and air pollution. Cold air and exercise can also start symptoms. Colds and the flu are very common triggers, especially in children. Even strong emotions, like stress, can start an attack. For Sara, the main trigger is exercise. So, a big part of asthma care is to know your triggers and avoid them.

**Key terms to stress:** **trigger** · **allergen** (AL-er-jen) · **pollen** (POL-un) · **mould** (MOHLD)

**Time:** 60 seconds

### Slide 8: Signs and Symptoms of Asthma

**What to say:**

> Now, how does asthma feel? There are four main symptoms. The first is wheezing, the whistling sound we heard about. The second is coughing, often at night or early in the morning. The third is chest tightness. Patients say it feels like a band around their chest. The fourth is shortness of breath. This means you feel you cannot get enough air. The key point is this: in asthma, symptoms come and go. But a sudden, severe attack can be an emergency. That's all about asthma from me. Now I will hand over to Presenter 2, who will tell you about COPD.

**Key terms to stress:** **symptoms** (SIMP-tumz) · **chest tightness** · **shortness of breath** · **emergency** (ih-MER-jen-see)

**Time:** 55 seconds

### Questions the audience might ask (Presenter 1)

**Q1. Can asthma be cured?**  
A: No, there is no cure yet. But with the right inhalers and by avoiding triggers, most people with asthma can live a normal, active life.

**Q2. Is asthma contagious?**  
A: No, you cannot catch asthma from another person. But a cold or the flu, which are contagious, can trigger an asthma attack.

## Presenter 2: COPD

**Slides 9–15 · Total time: 7:00 (420 seconds)**

### Slide 9: Meet Mr. Brown: A COPD Story

**What to say:**

> Thank you, Presenter 1. Now let me introduce our second patient, Mr. Brown. He is sixty-seven years old, and he smoked cigarettes for forty years. Every morning, he coughs and brings up phlegm. Phlegm is thick mucus that we cough up from the chest. Climbing stairs makes him breathless. He also feels tired most of the day. His breathing does not come and go like Sara's. It has slowly got worse over many years.

**Key terms to stress:** **phlegm** (FLEM) · **breathless** (BRETH-lus)

**Time:** 40 seconds

### Slide 10: What Is COPD?

**What to say:**

> Mr. Brown has COPD. COPD is an abbreviation. Doctors and nurses use it every day, so let's look at each letter. C is for chronic. Chronic means long-lasting. O is for obstructive. As we learned, this means the airflow is blocked. P is for pulmonary. Pulmonary means related to the lungs. And D is for disease. So, COPD is a long-lasting lung disease that blocks the airflow. Here are some key facts. First, the blockage is not fully reversible. This is very different from asthma. Second, COPD is progressive. Progressive means it slowly gets worse over time. And third, it usually starts after the age of forty. It is often found late, because it starts so slowly.

**Key terms to stress:** **chronic** (KRON-ik) · **pulmonary** (PUL-muh-nair-ee) · **progressive** (pruh-GRES-iv)

**Time:** 65 seconds

### Slide 11: Two Main Types of COPD

**What to say:**

> COPD has two main types, and most patients have a mix of both. The first type is chronic bronchitis. Remember, the bronchi are the large airway tubes. The ending "itis" means inflammation. So bronchitis is inflammation of the bronchi. The tubes become swollen, and they make too much mucus. The main sign is a cough with phlegm that lasts for months, year after year. The second type is emphysema. Emphysema means the alveoli, the tiny air sacs, are damaged. Their thin walls break down. Because of this, the lungs lose their elasticity. Elasticity is the ability to stretch and spring back, like a rubber band. Without it, the lungs cannot push the air out well. So the air gets trapped inside, and the patient feels short of breath. So remember: bronchitis affects the tubes, and emphysema affects the air sacs.

**Key terms to stress:** **bronchitis** (brong-KY-tis) · **emphysema** (em-fuh-SEE-muh) · **elasticity** (ee-las-TIS-ih-tee)

**Time:** 80 seconds

### Slide 12: What Causes COPD?

**What to say:**

> What causes COPD? The number one cause is smoking. According to the World Health Organization, in high-income countries, more than seventy percent of COPD cases are linked to smoking. But smoking is not the only cause. Second-hand smoke is also harmful. That means breathing in other people's smoke. In many poorer countries, a big cause is household air pollution. This is smoke from cooking or heating with wood, coal or other fuels inside the home. Outdoor air pollution, and dust and chemicals at work, can also damage the lungs. Frequent lung infections in childhood can play a role too. Finally, there is a rare genetic condition called alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency. It can cause COPD in young people, even if they never smoked.

**Key terms to stress:** **second-hand smoke** · **household air pollution** (puh-LOO-shun) · **alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency** (AL-fuh WUN an-tee-TRIP-sin dih-FISH-un-see)

**Time:** 70 seconds

### Slide 13: Symptoms of COPD

**What to say:**

> Now, let's look at the symptoms of COPD. The main symptom is shortness of breath. The medical word for this is dyspnea. Patients also have a chronic cough, and they cough up sputum. Sputum is another word for phlegm. Some patients also have wheezing, chest tightness, and they feel tired. Now look at the diagram. In COPD, breathlessness slowly gets worse. At first, a patient is breathless only on stairs or hills. Later, they are breathless when walking on flat ground. In severe COPD, even getting dressed can be hard. Many people think this is just "getting old", so they see a doctor late.

**Key terms to stress:** **dyspnea** (DISP-nee-uh) · **sputum** (SPYOO-tum) · **breathlessness** (BRETH-lus-nus)

**Time:** 60 seconds

### Slide 14: Flare-Ups (Exacerbations)

**What to say:**

> Sometimes, COPD suddenly gets worse. This is called a flare-up. The medical word is exacerbation. During a flare-up, the patient is more breathless than usual. They cough more, and they have more sputum. Sometimes the sputum changes colour to yellow or green. The most common causes are chest infections, like a cold or the flu, and air pollution. Flare-ups are dangerous. Serious ones need hospital care, sometimes with oxygen. Patients often need extra medicines, like steroids or antibiotics. Each flare-up can also leave the lungs a little weaker than before. That is why preventing them is so important.

**Key terms to stress:** **flare-up** (FLAIR-up) · **exacerbation** (ig-ZAS-er-BAY-shun)

**Time:** 55 seconds

### Slide 15: Why Should We Care?

**What to say:**

> So, why should we care about these diseases? Let's look at the numbers from the World Health Organization. In 2019, about two hundred and sixty-two million people had asthma. In the same year, asthma caused about four hundred and fifty-five thousand deaths. COPD is even more deadly. In 2023, it caused three point four million deaths. That makes it the third leading cause of death in the world. Most of these deaths happen in low- and middle-income countries. Now, Presenter 3 will explain how doctors diagnose and treat these diseases.

**Key terms to stress:** **262 million** (two hundred sixty-two million) · **455,000** (four hundred fifty-five thousand) · **3.4 million** (three point four million) · **leading cause of death**

**Time:** 50 seconds

### Questions the audience might ask (Presenter 2)

**Q1. If a patient stops smoking, does COPD go away?**  
A: No, the damage that is already there cannot be reversed. But stopping smoking slows the disease down a lot, and it is the most helpful thing a patient can do.

**Q2. Can a hookah (water pipe) or e-cigarettes cause COPD too?**  
A: Hookah smoke contains many of the same toxic chemicals as cigarette smoke, so it can damage the lungs too. The long-term risk of e-cigarettes is still being studied, but they are not considered safe for the lungs.

## Presenter 3: Diagnosis, treatment, comparison and quiz

**Slides 16–23 · Total time: 7:00 (420 seconds)**

### Slide 16: How Do Doctors Diagnose Them?

**What to say:**

> Thank you, Presenter 2. So, how do doctors find out if a patient has asthma or COPD? There are three main steps. Step one is to ask. The doctor takes a history. A history is the patient's story: their symptoms, if they smoke, their allergies, and illnesses in the family. Step two is to examine. The doctor listens to the chest with a stethoscope and may hear a wheeze. Step three is to test. The most important test is spirometry, a breathing test. Sometimes doctors also use a chest X-ray, or they measure the oxygen in the blood. Let's look at spirometry more closely.

**Key terms to stress:** **history** · **stethoscope** (STETH-uh-skohp) · **spirometry** (spy-ROM-uh-tree)

**Time:** 55 seconds

### Slide 17: Spirometry: The Breathing Test

**What to say:**

> Spirometry measures how much air you can blow out, and how fast. The patient takes a very deep breath in. Then they blow out into a machine, as hard and as fast as they can. The machine measures two numbers. The first is FEV1. It is the air you blow out in the first second. The second is FVC. It is all the air you blow out in total. Then we divide FEV1 by FVC. If the result is less than zero point seven, the airways are obstructed. After that, the patient uses a reliever inhaler and does the test again. Look at the chart. In asthma, the result improves a lot. In COPD, it improves only a little. This is how spirometry helps doctors tell the two diseases apart.

**Key terms to stress:** **FEV1** (eff-ee-vee-WUN) · **FVC** (eff-vee-SEE) · **obstructed** (ub-STRUK-tid) · **inhaler** (in-HAY-ler)

**Time:** 70 seconds

### Slide 18: Treating Asthma

**What to say:**

> Now let's talk about treatment, starting with asthma. Most asthma medicines are inhalers. An inhaler is a small device that sends the medicine straight into the lungs. The first type is the reliever inhaler. It works in minutes and relaxes the airway muscles, so we call it a bronchodilator, which means "airway opener". Patients use it when symptoms start. A common example is salbutamol. The second type is the controller, or preventer, inhaler. It contains a steroid, a medicine that calms the swelling. It works slowly, so patients use it every day, even when they feel well. An example is budesonide. Patients should also avoid triggers and follow an asthma action plan. This is a written plan that says what to do when symptoms get worse.

**Key terms to stress:** **reliever** (rih-LEE-ver) · **bronchodilator** (BRONG-koh-dy-LAY-ter) · **steroid** (STEER-oyd) · **salbutamol** (sal-BYOO-tuh-mol) · **budesonide** (byoo-DES-uh-nyde)

**Time:** 65 seconds

### Slide 19: Treating COPD

**What to say:**

> Now, COPD treatment. There are five main parts. Number one, and the most important: stop smoking. It is never too late to stop, and quitting slows down the damage. Number two: inhalers that open the airways, the bronchodilators. Number three: pulmonary rehabilitation. This is a program of exercise, breathing training and education. It helps patients do more in their daily life, and they feel less breathless. Number four: vaccines against the flu, pneumonia and COVID-19. They prevent infections and flare-ups. And number five: oxygen therapy, for patients with severe COPD and low oxygen levels. COPD cannot be cured. But treatment helps people breathe better and live better.

**Key terms to stress:** **rehabilitation** (REE-huh-bil-ih-TAY-shun) · **pneumonia** (noo-MOH-nee-uh) · **vaccines** (vak-SEENZ) · **therapy** (THAIR-uh-pee)

**Time:** 60 seconds

### Slide 20: Asthma vs COPD: Side by Side

**What to say:**

> Now let's put the two diseases side by side. Please look at the table as I explain. First, age. Asthma often starts in childhood. COPD usually starts after forty. Second, the main cause. Asthma is linked to allergies and triggers. COPD is mainly caused by smoking and other smoke. Third, the pattern of symptoms. In asthma, they come and go. In COPD, they slowly get worse. Fourth, the blockage. In asthma, it is mostly reversible. In COPD, it is not fully reversible. Finally, treatment. Asthma is treated mainly with a controller inhaler and a reliever. COPD is treated by stopping smoking and with bronchodilators. Of course, some patients have features of both diseases.

**Key terms to stress:** **reversible / not fully reversible** (rih-VER-suh-bul) · **features** (FEE-cherz)

**Time:** 65 seconds

### Slide 21: Quick Quiz: Asthma or COPD?

**What to say:**

> Now it's your turn! I will read four short sentences. Please call out the answer: asthma or COPD? Number one. A ten-year-old boy wheezes every time he plays with a cat. *[pause, then click]* Yes, asthma! Number two. A seventy-year-old ex-smoker coughs up phlegm every morning. *[click]* Correct, COPD. Number three. Her breathing test becomes almost normal after an inhaler. *[click]* Asthma, because it is reversible. Number four. The walls of his alveoli are damaged, and air is trapped. *[click]* That's emphysema, so it's COPD. Great job, everyone!

**Key terms to stress:** **ex-smoker** (EKS-smoh-ker)

**Time:** 50 seconds (the audience's answers fill the pauses)

### Slide 22: Take-Home Messages

**What to say:**

> To finish, here are our take-home messages. Asthma and COPD both block the airflow out of the lungs. Asthma has sensitive airways and triggers, and it is usually reversible. COPD is mostly caused by smoking. It slowly gets worse, and it is not fully reversible. Both diseases can be treated with the right inhalers. And remember: COPD can often be prevented. The best advice is simple. Don't smoke.

**Key terms to stress:** **take-home message** · **prevented** (prih-VEN-tid)

**Time:** 35 seconds

### Slide 23: Thank You!

**What to say:**

> That is the end of our presentation. Thank you very much for listening. We hope you learned something new today. We also want to thank our teacher for this opportunity. Now, we are happy to answer your questions.

**Key terms to stress:** none on this slide. Smile, slow down and look at the audience.

**Time:** 20 seconds

### Questions the audience might ask (Presenter 3)

**Q1. Can a person have both asthma and COPD?**  
A: Yes. Some adults, often people with asthma who smoke, have features of both diseases. Doctors then treat both problems together.

**Q2. Why is good inhaler technique so important?**  
A: If the technique is wrong, most of the medicine stays in the mouth instead of reaching the lungs. That is why doctors and nurses check the patient's technique at every visit.
